# Taste, Food, and Hunger: Initial Brainstorming

September 29, 2026 · Notes for future reflection and learning

**Status:** Exploring ideas. No interactive model has been selected or built. This document preserves the discussion, including ideas later narrowed or set aside. Proposed explanations and model behaviors are possibilities, not established findings.

## 1. Where the project began

My starting observation:

> “I noticed food tastes better depending on the context.”

I wanted to explore taste and the context of where and how we eat because the experience matters. I requested a project subfolder and asked to describe the experience more precisely before receiving a design or code.

The first question was:

> “Can you describe a specific time when food tasted better because of where or how you were eating, and what about that experience seemed to make the difference?”

My example:

> “One moment when a food tastes better because of where I was and how I was eating was today, when i didn't have lunch and my two oranges that I ate were satisfying and fulfilled my cravings for food at the moment.”

The concrete details are two oranges, a missed lunch, a craving for food, and a feeling of satisfaction. The setting, pace of eating, and specific flavor qualities have not yet been described.

## 2. The first project framing

The initial assignment brief shifted attention toward hunger as part of eating context. Its guiding question was:

> How does hunger influence the way I describe a food's taste and the satisfaction of eating it?

It proposed observing the same food on a few ordinary occasions, recording hunger and expectations beforehand, setting and company, sensory qualities, satisfaction, and a short reflection. The suggested deliverable was three entries and a comparison paragraph. Hunger differences would arise naturally; skipping meals was not part of the assignment.

An unresolved distinction emerged: **Did the oranges taste better, feel more satisfying, or both?** Differences in the food itself could also affect the experience.

This was an initial proposal, not a settled method. My later brief asks for a more visual, experiential direction and makes clear that hunger alone must not become the focus.

## 3. My interpretation and its assumption

The assumption I identified:

> “When you are hungry any food or snack will do the job of satisfying you for the time being.”

The supporting detail as I interpreted it:

> “When you eat SOMETHING your mind gets kinda tricked that you have some substance in you and for the time being you are less hungry.”

This preserves my interpretation, but separates two things:

- **Experience reported:** After eating the oranges, I felt less hungry and satisfied.
- **Explanation proposed:** Eating anything temporarily satisfies hunger because the mind is “tricked.”

The experience does not by itself establish that explanation or show that every food would have the same effect.

## 4. An alternative possibility

The oranges may have satisfied me because particular qualities—such as juiciness, sweetness, or texture—matched what I wanted at that moment. Those qualities were suggested possibilities, not sensory details I had reported.

Under this reading, eating something might help, but different foods might not feel equally satisfying.

**Observation that could distinguish the readings:** During ordinary moments of similar hunger, notice whether different snacks feel satisfying or whether I still want something else afterward.

**Question to ask someone:**

> “Think of a time you were hungry and ate a snack. Did your hunger ease, and did you feel satisfied—or were you still craving a different food?”

The useful distinction is between **feeling less hungry** and **having a craving fulfilled**. A casual comparison can suggest patterns, but would not isolate a single cause.

## 5. A poetic lens we considered

One possible poetic approach was **imagery**: using sensory details to make an experience vivid rather than simply naming it. This was one lens a poet might use, not a claim that all poets think alike.

Possible details to recall included the orange's scent, a burst of juice, or a pause while eating. None should be treated as something that definitely happened.

The new question was:

> “If you described the first bite and the last bite without using ‘hungry,’ ‘delicious,’ or ‘satisfied,’ how would their descriptions differ?”

This raised the possibility of attending to change across the eating experience. I later asked to leave out some of the language focus and make the project more experiential and visual. The poetic exercise remains here as part of the thinking process, not a required design feature.

## 6. My clarified design brief

**What I want someone to notice, understand, or explore:** The relationship between food and hunger.

**What I want to preserve:**

> “The experience of eating anything when hungry will temporarily satisfy you, regardless of what the food is.”

**What I want to leave out:** Some of the specific focus on language. I want the experience to be more visual and experiential.

**What would misrepresent my intention:** Focusing only on hunger.

**Scale:** Two small model possibilities, using only the elements needed to show the relationship, with one interaction each. No building yet.

The “any food temporarily satisfies” idea is the premise I want to explore. Representing it in a model would not make it a verified claim about everyone.

## 7. Two proposed interactive models

### Model 1: A bite and a pause

**Elements:** Three different food icons and a gently pulsing circle representing the eater's urge to eat.

**One interaction:** Click a food to eat it.

**Behavioral rule:** Any food briefly softens the pulse; the pulse gradually returns.

**What it reveals:** Different foods can share the role of providing temporary relief. Food and hunger appear together in a changing experience.

**What it obscures:** Differences in flavor, enjoyment, and how long particular foods might satisfy someone.

**Fit with my intention:** This is the more direct representation of my stated premise. The pulse and its return would be expressive design choices, not measurements of hunger or biological timing.

### Model 2: The same food, a different moment

**Elements:** One food icon, a hunger slider, and a soft halo around the food representing its anticipated appeal.

**One interaction:** Move the hunger slider.

**Behavioral rule:** As hunger increases, the food's halo grows.

**What it reveals:** The food stays the same while the eater's state changes its perceived appeal, connecting food, hunger, and experience.

**What it obscures:** Actual eating and temporary satisfaction.

**Fit with my intention:** This explores the moment before eating. It introduces an additional possibility to test—that greater hunger makes a food more appealing—and does not directly preserve the whole temporary-satisfaction premise.

**Decision still open:** Neither model has been chosen. The first fits the stated premise more directly; the second explores what leads up to eating.

## 8. Claims and uncertainties to revisit

No external factual research has been completed for these notes. The following ideas need verification before being presented as general explanations:

- Eating “tricks the mind” into feeling less hungry.
- Any food will temporarily satisfy any hungry person, regardless of the food.
- Greater hunger necessarily increases a food's appeal.
- Different foods would produce equivalent relief or a predictable return of hunger.

The possibility that the oranges' sensory qualities matched my craving also remains an interpretation to explore. My reported experience can stand on its own without proving these explanations.

## 9. What this discussion has helped clarify

- The project began with taste and eating context, then moved toward the relationship between food and hunger.
- “Tastes better,” “less hungry,” “enjoyable,” and “satisfied” may describe different parts of the experience. Their relationship is still open.
- The food needs to remain central; a display of hunger alone would miss my intention.
- A small visual interaction can express a premise while leaving out important differences. Those omissions should stay visible in my reflection.
- I prefer an experiential direction over a language-centered activity.
